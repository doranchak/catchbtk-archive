To the best of my knowledge, the only phone call the police have acknowledged BTK ever made to them was the "Nancy Fox" call of December 1977 as heard on this website. For a guy who started killing several years earlier, that always struck me as odd.

Of course, the police (ALL police, not just in Wichita) are rather notorious for withholding information on unsolved cases, especially high-profile cases such as BTK. In fact, it's not exactly a rarity for the police to actually lie to the public/news media about certain facets of a case.

We know that BTK killed several members of the Otero family in January 1974, as well as Kathryn Bright in April 1974. In October 1974 BTK mailed a letter to the Wichita Eagle newspaper, his alleged first communication.

Here's what I believe the "Paging Sherlock Holmes" article indicates:

Soon after the Bright murder, just as he would do in December 1977, BTK called the Wichita police and reported his crime. He probably included details of the Otero murders as well, thus convincing the police he was responsible for all. The call was probably brief. Wanting more dialogue without alarming the public and not knowing how else to reach out to BTK, the police took out a personal ad asking him to call again.

Communicating through personal ads was/is a rather famous way for police agencies to solicit information from informants/criminals and vice-versa; see "Dirty Harry" from 1971. In fact, "Dirty Harry" was a film based on the serial killer known as the Zodiac. BTK directly copied several elements of the Zodiac's methodology. Perhaps the Wichita police knew this and that's why they chose placing an ad.

It's also not uncommon for the police to solicit the cooperation from the news media to generate new information on a particular case: "Do us a favor and give lots of publicity to the date and location of the victim's funeral; we'll have it staked out in case someone unusual shows up." The FBI's ex-serial killer profiler John Douglas has written about that successful practice several times in his books. Such a technique would also explain why the Associated Press picked up such a silly, local, Wichita-based, non-newsworthy story and distribute it all over the country. (I actually found the "Sherlock Holmes" article in an Ohio newspaper of all places.)

If my assumptions are correct and the personal ad was from the Wichita police to BTK, it means he has made more than one phone call. It gives BTK a motive for making his future contact with the Eagle newspaper. After all, he was after attention and his call to the police was fruitless in that regard. And finally, it's one more detail the Wichita police have held back.

By no means am I anti-Wichita police. However, after 30 years I believe a new approach is necessary. Sharing more information with the public can only help the chances of the police identifying the BTK strangler.

I intend to find more materials such as the "Sherlock Holmes" article and post them at catchBTK.com for the public to educate themselves.
